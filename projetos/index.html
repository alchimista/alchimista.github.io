understanding-cc-licenses

Working with open educational resources and digital collections means navigating the sometimes confusing world of Creative Commons licenses and public domain designations. After three years of working with partners on publications, I've learned that understanding how these licenses work—and whom they restrict—is fundamental to effective collaboration and resource sharing.

The Three Layers: How CC Licenses Actually Work

One of Creative Commons' most elegant innovations is the three-layer design that makes licenses simultaneously legally enforceable, human-readable, and machine-discoverable.

Layer 1: Legal Code

This is the actual contract—the legally precise text written in formal legal language that holds up in court. It defines the exact terms of use with all the necessary legal terminology and provisions. While most people never read this layer, it's the foundation that makes CC licenses legally valid worldwide.

Layer 2: Commons Deed (Human-Readable)

This is the summary most of us actually read. Written in plain language, it explains what users can and cannot do with the work. Think of it as the "user manual" for the license. When you see those familiar license badges on websites, clicking them takes you to this layer.

Layer 3: Machine-Readable Code

Hidden metadata embedded in the work allows search engines, repositories, and other technologies to automatically recognize and understand the license terms. This is what makes CC-licensed works discoverable through tools like Creative Commons Search and why search engines can filter by usage rights.

This three-layer approach ensures that CC licenses work for lawyers, everyday users, and technology platforms simultaneously.

The Building Blocks: Four License Elements

Creative Commons licenses are constructed from four basic elements that can be combined in different ways. Every CC license includes Attribution (BY), and the other three elements are optional additions:

Attribution (BY)

Users must give appropriate credit to the creator, provide a link to the license, and indicate if changes were made. This element appears in all six CC licenses—it's the foundation of the system. Proper attribution respects creators while enabling sharing.

NonCommercial (NC)

This restricts use to non-commercial purposes. Users can freely use the work for education, research, and personal projects, but cannot use it for commercial gain. However, there's an important nuance: the original creator (licensor) can always separately license or sell their own work commercially—the NC restriction only applies to licensees.

ShareAlike (SA)

If users create derivative works, they must distribute them under the same license as the original. This creates a "copyleft" effect, ensuring that adaptations remain open. Think of Wikipedia—all its content is CC BY-SA, meaning improvements must be shared back with the same license.

NoDerivatives (ND)

Users can copy and redistribute the work but cannot create adaptations or modifications. The work must be shared in its original form. Note that ND and SA are mutually exclusive—a license can have one or the other, but not both.

The Six CC Licenses: From Most to Least Permissive

Combining these elements creates six standard licenses, arranged here from most to least open:

CC BY (Attribution)

The most permissive license. Others can distribute, remix, adapt, and build upon your work, even commercially, as long as they credit you. This is the recommended license for maximum dissemination and impact, and it's the standard for open access scholarly articles.

CC BY-SA (Attribution-ShareAlike)

Others can remix and build upon your work even commercially, but must credit you and license new creations under identical terms. Wikipedia uses this license, creating a growing commons of freely available knowledge.

CC BY-NC (Attribution-NonCommercial)

Others can remix and adapt your work non-commercially. Derivative works don't need to be licensed on the same terms, but all uses must be non-commercial and give credit.

CC BY-NC-SA (Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike)

Combines non-commercial restrictions with the ShareAlike requirement. Others can build upon your work non-commercially, but must credit you and license new creations identically.

CC BY-ND (Attribution-NoDerivatives)

Others can redistribute your work commercially or non-commercially with credit, but cannot modify it. Useful when you want wide distribution but want to maintain the work's integrity.

CC BY-NC-ND (Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives)

The most restrictive CC license. Others can download and share your work with credit, but cannot change it or use it commercially. While this feels "safe," it significantly limits educational reuse and adaptation.

Beyond Licensing: The Two Public Domain Tools

It's crucial to understand that Creative Commons also provides two tools that are NOT licenses—they don't grant permissions because no permission is needed.

CC0 (CC Zero): Public Domain Dedication

A tool for creators to waive all their copyright and related rights, dedicating their work to the public domain. To the extent legally possible, the creator surrenders all copyright worldwide. There are no conditions whatsoever—not even attribution is required, though it's always appreciated.

When to use CC0: When you want to place your own work into the public domain with no strings attached. This is increasingly popular for research data, government documents, and factual databases where you want to maximize reuse without barriers.

PDM (Public Domain Mark): Identifying What's Already Free

A label for marking works that are already in the public domain—usually because copyright has expired, been forfeited, or was never applicable. This is not a legal instrument but a standardized way to communicate that a work is free to use.

When to use PDM: When you want to clearly identify and label works already in the public domain, making them easier for others to discover and use confidently. Libraries and archives use this extensively for digitized historical materials.

Key Differences from CC Licenses

Public domain tools differ fundamentally from licenses:

  • They indicate no permission is needed, rather than granting specific permissions
  • There are no restrictions or requirements whatsoever
  • Once in the public domain, works remain there permanently
  • The four license elements (BY, NC, SA, ND) don't apply
  • CC0 is for dedication; PDM is for identification

A Critical Issue: Exceptions and Limitations to Copyright

One of the most important—but often misunderstood—aspects of CC licenses is how they interact with existing copyright exceptions and limitations.

What Are Exceptions and Limitations?

These are legal provisions that allow certain uses of copyrighted works without permission. In the US, this includes "fair use" for purposes like criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research. Other countries have similar provisions called "fair dealing" or specific educational exceptions.

The Key Principle: Exceptions Always Apply

Creative Commons licenses do not reduce, limit, or restrict any rights under exceptions and limitations to copyright. These rights exist independently and are preserved regardless of the license applied.

This means:

Users retain all exception rights. Even if a work is licensed CC BY-NC-ND (the most restrictive), users can still make fair use of it under applicable copyright law.

Educational use continues. Teachers and researchers can use CC-licensed works under educational exceptions, and the CC license often makes this even easier by granting explicit additional permission beyond what the law already allows.

Criticism and commentary remain protected. You can quote from and comment on CC-licensed works under fair use or fair dealing, even if the license is NC or ND.

CC licenses provide baseline permissions. They establish a minimum set of permissions that work alongside—not instead of—existing copyright exceptions. Think of them as adding to what the law already permits, never subtracting from it.

International variations are respected. Different countries have different exceptions and limitations, and CC licenses respect all of them.

Practical Implication

If a use would be permitted under fair use or another copyright exception, a CC license cannot prohibit it. The license grants additional permissions beyond what copyright law already allows—it's always additive, never restrictive of existing rights.

Application to Institutional Work

For Educational Institutions

OER Development: Choose CC BY or CC BY-SA for maximum reusability and adaptation. Avoid NC and ND restrictions unless absolutely necessary, as they significantly limit educational use.

Research Publications: CC BY has become the gold standard for open access scholarly articles, required by many funders and publishers.

Student Work: Help students understand their options for licensing their creative outputs, empowering them to share their work on their own terms.

Institutional Repositories: Clearly mark public domain materials with PDM. Don't add unnecessary restrictions to materials that are already free to use. Consider encouraging CC0 for research data.

For Libraries and Archives

Digitized Collections: Use PDM for materials already in the public domain. As we discussed earlier regarding that 1936 edition of The House of Mirth, institutions should ideally provide granular rights information distinguishing between elements with different copyright statuses.

Composite Works: When a work contains both public domain and copyrighted elements (like a public domain novel with a new introduction), best practice is element-by-element labeling:

  • Novel text: Public Domain (published 1905)
  • Introduction: CC BY-NC-SA or In Copyright
  • Images/illustrations: [Separate rights statement]

Finding Aids and Metadata: Consider licensing your descriptive metadata under CC BY or even CC0 to enable maximum reuse and interoperability.

Rights Statements: Provide clear, accurate information about copyright status. While there's no legal obligation to update metadata when works enter the public domain, there's a strong ethical responsibility to maintain accurate rights information.

Common Institutional Scenarios

Adapting OER: If the original is CC BY-SA, your adaptation must also be CC BY-SA. Plan accordingly.

Commercial Partnerships: Ensure NC-licensed materials aren't used commercially by partners or in revenue-generating programs.

Creating Databases: Consider CC0 for factual data and metadata, which may not be copyrightable anyway and benefits from maximum reusability.

Historical Materials: Research copyright status carefully before applying licenses. Don't inadvertently restrict access to public domain works.

What I Wish I'd Known Three Years Ago

The distinction between what licensors can do versus what licensees can do has been my biggest learning curve. The licensor (original copyright holder) always retains all rights and can separately license or sell their work however they choose. The restrictions in a CC license only apply to licensees—the people using the work.

This means when you license your work CC BY-NC, you're not preventing yourself from commercial use—you're only restricting others. And once you've applied a CC license, while you can stop distributing under that license, anyone who already obtained the work under those terms can continue using it forever. CC licenses are irrevocable.

Understanding these fundamentals has made collaboration with partners clearer and helped prevent the confusion I've sometimes created around how licenses restrict different parties.

Moving Forward with Confidence

Creative Commons licenses and public domain tools provide a flexible, legally sound framework for sharing and reusing creative and educational works. By understanding the three layers, four elements, six licenses, and two public domain tools—along with how they interact with existing copyright exceptions—institutions can make informed decisions that balance openness with appropriate protection.

The goal isn't always maximum openness; it's choosing the right tool for your specific situation and clearly communicating rights information to users. Whether you're creating OER, digitizing collections, or helping students license their work, understanding these fundamentals empowers better decision-making in service of the open culture mission.
